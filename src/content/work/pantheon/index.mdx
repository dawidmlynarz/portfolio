## My role

I joined Pantheon through XWP as a Design Systems Designer. **The goal was a design system Pantheon could own and keep growing after I left.** I set up the foundations, pitched the system to stakeholders, and helped put together the team on Pantheon’s side that would look after it.

We started small, with **a design system for the marketing site**. When that worked, we grew it into **a design system for the whole product**. We wrote the documentation for both ourselves, I mentored the designers, and I worked with the engineers to get the components into Storybook.

### Key achievements

- Set up the foundations (grid, typography, spacing and color) for the marketing site and the product.
- Pitched the design system in a series of stakeholder calls, which led Pantheon to form a dedicated design system team.
- Shipped the marketing site design system first, then grew it into a full product design system.
- Wrote the documentation for both systems in-house, from foundations to components and patterns.
- Trained the new team through mentoring, reviews and hands-on sessions.
- Worked with engineering to bring the components into Storybook, so design and code used the same source.

## Project Background

Pantheon is a WebOps platform for Drupal and WordPress, used by developers, agencies and marketing teams. As the company grew, **the marketing site and the product started to look and behave differently**, and teams kept solving the same problems in their own ways.

A shared design system was the obvious answer, but nobody owned it yet and not everyone was convinced it was worth the effort. So instead of redesigning everything at once, we decided to try it on the marketing site first and bring it to the product later.

### Objectives

- Make the marketing site and the product consistent.
- Help designers and developers ship faster.
- Improve how design and engineering work together.
- Write clear guidance for every foundation and component.

<Figure alt="Audit of inconsistent buttons, type and colors across Pantheon’s marketing site and product." />

## The Challenge

Designing components was the easy part. **The hard part was finding the system a home.** A design system needs people who own it, and at the start Pantheon had nobody in that role. Without buy-in and a team, whatever we built would slowly go out of date.

The foundations also had to work in two quite different places, roomy marketing pages and dense product screens, and the system had to exist in code, not only in Figma.

## Getting buy-in

I presented the design system to Pantheon’s stakeholders over several calls. I kept it close to problems they already had: experiences that didn’t match, slow delivery, and lots of back-and-forth between design and engineering.

**After those calls, Pantheon formed a dedicated design system team.** That changed everything, because the system now had owners inside the company.

<Figure alt="Stakeholder presentation slides making the case for a design system." />

## Marketing site first, then the product

**The marketing site was our pilot.** It was a smaller surface with a clear owner and it carried the brand, so it was a good place to test the foundations, the first components and our way of working before we touched the product.

Once the team was comfortable with the marketing design system, **we grew it into a design system for the product**. The product used the same foundations with tighter type and spacing, plus many more components for forms, tables, navigation and the dashboard.

<Figure alt="Marketing site pages built with the pilot design system, next to product screens built with the full product design system." />

## Foundations for marketing and product

I set up the foundations so marketing and product could share one language and still feel right in their own context.

- **Grid.** A fluid grid with 4 columns on mobile, going up to 6 and 12 on bigger screens.
- **Typography.** A modular scale in Poppins: 1.25 (Major Third) for marketing and 1.2 (Minor Third) for the product.
- **Spacing.** A fluid scale with a 1.25 ratio, where 1rem goes from 16px on small screens to 24px on large ones. Marketing gets more room, the product is more compact.
- **Color.** One palette with primary, accent, status, neutral and decorative colors for the whole brand.

## Documentation

We wrote the documentation for both systems ourselves instead of leaving notes in Figma. Each foundation, component and pattern explains when to use it, how to place it, and what to avoid. The links and buttons pages, for example, cover when to use each one, when not to, and every style and state.

Writing the docs first meant we had to agree on decisions before anything was built. It also gave developers a clear reference when they built the components in Storybook.

## Training the team

A design system only lasts if the people who own it understand it. I mentored Pantheon’s designers through reviews and pairing, and **made sure they could run the system without me**.

We also turned that knowledge into training for the wider team:

- **Product design toolkit**, a self-paced Figma onboarding.
- **Components**, covering linking, grids, variants, auto layout, spacers, tables and governance.
- **Figma tips and tricks** for everyday work.

<Figure alt="Training session slides from the Figma components workshop." />

## Governance and Communication

We set up regular check-ins and feedback between design and engineering. The new team took over maintaining both design systems as Pantheon’s products changed.

<Figure alt="Pantheon components in Storybook next to their Figma counterparts." />

## Key Takeaways

Starting with the marketing site was the right call. It proved the foundations and the process, so growing it into a product design system felt like the next step rather than a big new ask.

What I value most from this project is the team Pantheon built around the system. Good foundations, clear docs and a close link to engineering matter, but a design system keeps going because someone owns it.
