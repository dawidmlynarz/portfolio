## My role

I joined Pantheon through XWP as a Design Systems Designer, with one goal: **give Pantheon a design system it could own and grow on its own**. I established the foundations, made the case for the system to stakeholders, and helped form the team on Pantheon’s side that would carry it forward.

We started with a pilot, **a design system for Pantheon’s marketing site**, and once it had proven itself we grew it into **a full design system for the product**. Along the way we built the documentation for both systems ourselves, I guided and mentored the designers, and I worked with the engineering team to integrate the components into Storybook.

### Key achievements

- Established the design system foundations for Pantheon’s marketing site and product: grid, typography, spacing and color.
- Sold the idea of a design system in a series of stakeholder calls, which led to a dedicated design system team being formed at Pantheon.
- Shipped the pilot, the marketing site design system, then scaled it into a full product design system.
- Built the documentation for both design systems ourselves, from foundations to components and patterns.
- Passed on the know-how to the new team through mentoring, reviews and hands-on training.
- Collaborated with engineering to integrate the components into Storybook, so designers and developers worked from the same source.

## Project Background

Pantheon is a WebOps platform for Drupal and WordPress, used by developers, agencies and marketing teams. As the company grew, **the brand and the user experience drifted apart**: the marketing site and the product each looked and behaved a little differently, and every team solved the same problems in its own way.

A shared design system was the obvious fix, but there was no team for it and no agreement yet that it was worth the investment. Rather than redesigning everything at once, the plan was to prove the approach on the marketing site first, and then bring it to the product.

### Objectives

- Create consistency across Pantheon’s marketing site and product.
- Improve efficiency and time to market for designers and developers.
- Strengthen collaboration between design and engineering.
- Reduce ambiguity with clear, written guidance for every foundation and component.

<Figure alt="Audit of inconsistent buttons, type and colors across Pantheon’s marketing site and product." />

## The Challenge

**The hardest part was not the components, it was getting the system a home.** A design system needs owners, and at the start Pantheon had none. Without buy-in and a team, anything we built would slowly fall out of date.

On top of that, the foundations had to serve two very different contexts, airy marketing pages and dense product screens, and the system had to land in code, not only in Figma.

## Selling the idea

I took the design system to Pantheon’s stakeholders in a series of calls, framing it around the problems they already felt: inconsistent experiences, slow delivery, and too much back-and-forth between design and engineering.

**Those conversations led to a dedicated design system team being formed on Pantheon’s side.** That was the turning point: from then on the system had owners inside the company, not only an external designer.

<Figure alt="Stakeholder presentation slides making the case for a design system." />

## Marketing first, then product

**The marketing site was our pilot.** It was a contained surface with a clear owner, it carried the brand most visibly, and it let us test the foundations, the first components and the way of working before touching the product.

Once the marketing design system was in use and the team was confident with it, **we scaled it into a full product design system**. The product reused the same foundations, with denser type and spacing and a much larger set of components for forms, tables, navigation and the dashboard.

<Figure alt="Marketing site pages built with the pilot design system, next to product screens built with the full product design system." />

## Foundations for two contexts

I established the foundations so marketing and product could share one language and still feel right in their own context.

- **Grid.** A fluid grid starting at 4 columns on mobile and scaling to 6 and 12 columns on larger screens.
- **Typography.** A modular scale in Poppins, with a 1.25 ratio (Major Third) for marketing and a tighter 1.2 ratio (Minor Third) for product.
- **Spacing.** A fluid spacing scale with a 1.25 ratio, where 1rem grows from 16px on the smallest screens to 24px on the largest. Marketing spacing is airier, product spacing more compact.
- **Color.** A unified palette with primary, accent, status, neutral and decorative colors shared across the brand.

<Figure alt="Foundations: the fluid grid, the marketing and product type scales, and the spacing scale." />

## Documentation we built ourselves

We wrote and built the documentation for both design systems ourselves, instead of leaving it as notes in Figma. Every foundation, component and pattern came with guidance: when to use it, how to place it, and do’s and don’ts. Forms, for example, were documented with alignment and placement rules, stacked and horizontal layouts, and spacing annotations.

**Writing the documentation first forced decisions to be agreed before they were built**, and it gave developers an unambiguous reference when they implemented the components in Storybook.

## Passing on the know-how

A system only lasts if the people who own it know how it works. I guided and mentored Pantheon’s designers through reviews and pairing, and **made sure the team had everything it needed to run the system without me**.

That knowledge was turned into training sessions and workshops for the wider team:

- **Product design toolkit**, a self-paced Figma onboarding.
- **Components**, covering linking, grids, variants, auto layout, spacers, tables and governance.
- **Figma tips and tricks** to speed up everyday work.

<Figure alt="Training session slides from the Figma components workshop." />

## Governance and Communication

To keep the system alive, we set up regular communication and feedback loops between design and engineering, and the new team took over maintaining and updating both design systems as Pantheon’s products evolved.

<Figure alt="Pantheon components in Storybook next to their Figma counterparts." />

## Key Takeaways

Starting small paid off. The marketing pilot proved the foundations and the way of working, which made scaling into a full product design system a natural next step rather than a hard sell.

For me, the biggest win was not a component. It was **leaving behind a team that owns the system**. A design system built on solid foundations, clear documentation and a direct line to engineering is the one that keeps scaling after you leave.
