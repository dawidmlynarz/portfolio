## My role

I joined Pantheon through XWP to **lead the design system initiative and the designers working on it**. My job was to set the direction, keep the work moving towards a system engineers could actually ship, and help the team grow while doing it.

I worked closely with Pantheon’s design and engineering teams, mentored the designers who built and documented the components, and partnered with developers to bring the Figma components to life in Storybook. **The first product to run on the new system was Pantheon’s documentation site, our pilot.**

### Key achievements

- Led a documentation-first design system for Pantheon’s marketing site and product, from foundations to components.
- Mentored and coached the designers on the team through reviews, pairing and hands-on training.
- Shipped the pilot: in October 2023 [Pantheon’s docs site moved to the new design system](https://docs.pantheon.io/release-notes/2023/10/design-system-integration-docs), giving it a more consistent, polished and accessible experience.
- Helped integrate the components into Storybook, so designers and developers worked from the same source.

## Project Background

Pantheon is a WebOps platform for Drupal and WordPress, used by developers, agencies and marketing teams. As the product suite grew, **the brand and the user experience drifted apart**: marketing pages, the product dashboard and the documentation each looked and behaved a little differently.

Pantheon decided to fix this with a shared design system. Rather than redesigning everything at once, we agreed to prove it on one real surface first, and then roll it out further.

### Objectives

- Create consistency across Pantheon’s marketing site, product and documentation.
- Improve efficiency and time to market for designers and developers.
- Strengthen collaboration between design and engineering.
- Reduce ambiguity with clear, written guidance for every foundation and component.

## The Challenge

A design system only works if people use it, and the team building this one was new to working this way. **The challenge was to build a system that could serve two very different contexts, airy marketing pages and dense product screens, while growing the people building it.**

On top of that, the system had to land in code, not only in Figma. That meant Figma and Storybook had to stay in step, and the work had to be planned around engineering capacity.

## A pilot before a rollout

I pushed for a pilot instead of a big-bang launch. **Pantheon’s documentation site was the right first candidate**: it is used every day by developers, has a lot of real content, and touches typography, navigation, forms and code samples, which stress-test any system.

The pilot gave us a clear goal and a deadline, let engineering integrate the components on a contained surface, and gave us real feedback before we asked other teams to adopt the system.

## Foundations for two contexts

We built the foundations so marketing and product could share one language and still feel right in their own context.

- **Grid.** A fluid grid starting at 4 columns on mobile and scaling to 6 and 12 columns on larger screens.
- **Typography.** A modular scale in Poppins, with a 1.25 ratio (Major Third) for marketing and a tighter 1.2 ratio (Minor Third) for product.
- **Spacing.** A fluid spacing scale with a 1.25 ratio, where 1rem grows from 16px on the smallest screens to 24px on the largest. Marketing spacing is airier, product spacing more compact.
- **Color.** A unified palette with primary, accent, status, neutral and decorative colors shared across the brand.

## Documentation first

Every foundation, component and pattern came with guidance: when to use it, how to place it, and do’s and don’ts. Forms, for example, were documented with alignment and placement rules, stacked and horizontal layouts, and spacing annotations.

**Writing the documentation first forced us to agree on decisions before they were built**, and it gave developers an unambiguous reference when they implemented the components in Storybook.

## Mentoring the team

A large part of my role was people, not pixels. I guided the designers through the work with regular reviews and pair-design sessions, and **helped them grow from building screens to building a system**.

Together we ran training sessions and workshops for the wider team:

- **Product design toolkit**, a self-paced Figma onboarding.
- **Components**, covering linking, grids, variants, auto layout, spacers, tables and governance.
- **Figma tips and tricks** to speed up everyday work.

## Governance and Communication

To keep the system alive after launch, we set up regular communication and feedback loops between design and engineering, and a dedicated team to maintain and update the system as Pantheon’s products evolved.

## Key Takeaways

The pilot did what it was meant to do: it put the design system in front of real users on Pantheon’s docs site, and gave the team a proven foundation to roll out across the platform.

For me, this project confirmed that **leading a design system is as much about growing the team as it is about components**. A system built by confident designers, with clear documentation and a direct line to engineering, is the one that keeps scaling after you leave.
