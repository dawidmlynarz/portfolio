## My role

My role at XWP was split down the middle: **half Principal Product Designer, half Design Lead**. I reported to the CDO and, at the team’s peak, led five designers: three seniors, one mid-level and one junior, each embedded in a different client project.

The goal was not to fix one broken thing. It was to **build a design practice inside an engineering-heavy company**: show what design adds, give designers a process and tools that fit how XWP delivers, and raise the quality of design across the company.

### Key achievements

- Built the design team’s hiring process from scratch, adapted from XWP’s engineering hiring.
- Wrote the design process and onboarding documentation, based on the Double Diamond, and paired every new designer with an onboarding buddy.
- Ran the team’s rituals: bi-weekly 1:1s, a weekly design sync and design critiques.
- Owned quarterly goals, career growth and performance reviews for every designer on the team.
- Built Astro Kit, a Figma design system compatible with the WordPress ecosystem, and led its growth into Launchpad, the boilerplate engineering teams start every project from.
- Introduced design discovery, a project framework and visual QA by designers on every new client project.

## Project Background

XWP builds and scales WordPress platforms for large publishers and brands, and its culture grew out of engineering. Design was often seen as a step before development rather than part of delivery, and every designer worked alone on a different client project.

That made design hard to see from the outside, and hard to keep consistent from the inside. There was no shared design process or toolkit, and no path for hiring or growing designers.

### Objectives

- Make the value of design visible to engineering, delivery and leadership.
- Give designers one process and one toolkit, whatever client they work for.
- Build a hiring, onboarding and growth path for the design team.
- Offer design as a service clients can buy, not only a step in delivery.

## The Challenge

**Building a design team in an engineering-heavy company is mostly about influence.** I had no mandate to change how delivery worked, so every change had to earn its place with the people who ran it.

I worked closely with the CTO, engineering leads, project managers, HR, and the marketing and branding teams. The aim was to show, project by project, that early design work made delivery faster, not slower.

## A team that works as one

Every designer sat in a different client project, so the team had to be built on purpose. I set up a rhythm that kept us connected without pulling anyone away from their client:

- **Bi-weekly 1:1s** with every designer.
- **A weekly design sync** to share progress, blockers and decisions across projects.
- **Design critiques** where work was reviewed by the whole team before it reached the client.

I was also responsible for each designer’s **quarterly goals, career growth and performance reviews**, so growth conversations were tied to the work, not saved for once a year.

## Hiring and onboarding from the ground up

XWP had a mature hiring process for engineers and none for designers. Rather than invent a parallel one, **I adapted the engineering process to the designer role**: a portfolio review, a design task, structured interview stages and scorecards, so designers were assessed as consistently as engineers.

New designers started with **documentation of the design process** that I wrote and aligned with the team, based on the Double Diamond. Each one was paired with **an onboarding buddy**, so they had someone to ask from day one.

## Astro Kit and Launchpad

The biggest lever was the toolkit. **I built Astro Kit, a Figma design system made for the WordPress ecosystem**, and led its expansion. Its foundations map to WordPress presets: a fluid type scale built for `min()`, `max()` and `clamp()`, spacing and inset tokens, and color tokens with aliases engineers could use directly.

Astro Kit came with a project structure for Figma, so every client project was organised the same way: one design system library with foundations, components and templates, next to the working files and a single source file shared with the client.

Then engineering picked it up. **Astro Kit was forged into Launchpad, adopted by the development teams and used as the boilerplate for every new project.** Design and code started from the same foundations, which made kick-off and handoff much faster.

<Figure alt="Astro Kit next to Launchpad: the same tokens in Figma and in the WordPress boilerplate." />

## A framework for every client

On top of the toolkit, I introduced a repeatable way to run design on client work:

- **Design discovery** at the start of each project, to agree on goals and scope before anything was drawn.
- **A design system and project framework** for every new client, built on Astro Kit.
- **Faster, simpler handoff**, because design and code shared the same tokens and structure.
- **Visual QA by designers** before release, so what shipped matched what was designed.

## Key Takeaways

In an engineering-led company, the way to grow design is to make delivery better, not to ask for a seat at the table. Astro Kit becoming Launchpad is the moment that happened: a design tool turned into the starting point for engineering.

For me, the lasting result was the practice itself: a team with a shared process, a hiring and growth path, and a toolkit every project starts from.
